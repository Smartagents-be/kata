Cost

Price is per token, and the tiers are further apart than most people expect. Call the small one a single unit. The middle tier costs roughly three of those, the top tier roughly five Output runs about five times the price of input in every tier, so a model that answers at length costs more than the same model answering briefly.

The bottom row is not one of the three. Providers also ship a frontier model, priced past the top tier and kept for work the top tier cannot hold together. It is a ceiling rather than a fourth tier.

Those ratios hold up better than the numbers do. Providers cut prices and ship new versions, and the top tier stays the top tier. Learn the shape rather than the price list.

API vs subscription

Two arrangements, and the harness is where you pick one. Hand it an API key and the provider bills you per token, at the rates above, for exactly what went up and what came back. Sign in with a subscription instead and the same usage comes off a plan you paid for before you started.

That choice also decides which models you can reach. A subscription buys you into one provider's own program, so you get their family and nothing else. A key is looser. Most open-source harnesses take one, and that is what lets you point the same tool at whichever tier you like.

The five-hour window

Your provider might give you a session limit, and it is usually a sliding window of five hours. Your first message opens one. Everything you send lands inside it, and when the five hours are up the next thing you type opens the next window. This is a clock, not the pile of turns the session unit is about. The two share a word by accident.

Over the top of it there is usually a weekly ceiling as well, so a heavy Monday is still a Monday on Thursday. You do not have to guess where you stand on either. Ask the harness and it prints the window you are in, when it resets, and how much of the week went with it.

What starts the clock is your first message, not your first real question. Open a window at half past nine while you are reading mail and it shuts at half past two, in the middle of the afternoon you wanted it for. You did not spend those five hours. They went past you.

Work out when you take your break and subtract five hours. Say hello at that hour, by typing the word or from a repeating task you set up to send it for you, and the window you just opened runs right up to the moment you stand up. Say hello again on your way out to the break, and the second one covers the break and every hour left in the day

Speed

The small tier answers roughly four to five times faster than the middle one, and the top tier is the slowest of the three. On one question that is a few seconds and you will not care. Inside a loop that runs a hundred times, it is the difference between a job you watch and a job you leave running.

Here is where quick stops being cheap. A fast wrong answer costs you the run it broke plus the second run to fix it, and you paid for both. The small tier wins when the task is specified tightly enough that there is nothing left to get wrong.

Reasoning level

Two dials, and they are easy to confuse. The reasoning level decides how long one model thinks before it answers. The tier decides which model is doing the thinking. Turning reasoning up on the small tier costs you tokens and time, and it does not hand you the top tier.

Or let it pick for you

You do not have to make this call every time. The coordinator in the previous unit is this choice handed to the machine: the expensive model works out what has to happen, and the parts go to cheaper ones. Top rate for deciding, a fraction of it for doing.

There is a reason that split works as well as it does. Providers fine-tune the smaller tiers on output from the larger ones, so an expensive model writing the brief is writing for something trained on its own answers. Working out what the cheap model should do is the half it is best at.
