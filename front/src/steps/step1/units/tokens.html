The model does not read your words. Before anything reaches it your text is cut into chunks: common words survive whole, rare ones break into pieces, and every chunk is swapped for a number. Those chunks are tokens.

This unit is not about what goes into an agent's head. It is about what the contents are counted in. Everything an agent knows when it answers you is text, and text is limited, priced and re-read by the token. Not by the word, and not by the character. Get the unit wrong and every number in this step is wrong with it.

For ordinary English, four or five characters go into one token, so a page of prose runs to a few hundred.

Text does not split into words

A tokeniser has a fixed vocabulary, built once from a pile of text and frozen. It cuts your input into the longest pieces that vocabulary already knows. Common English words are in there whole, and so is the space in front of them. Anything the vocabulary has not seen often enough gets taken apart until what is left does fit.

This is why a pasted stack trace, a log full of ids, or a wall of minified JSON costs several times what its length suggests. Prose is the cheapest thing you can put in front of a model. Identifiers, hashes and ids are the dearest, and a codebase is made of those.

It writes one token at a time

A model does one thing. Given everything it has been handed so far, it scores the tokens that could come next, and one of them is taken. That token is stuck on the end, and the whole lot goes back in. Again.

There is no sentence being written out here. There are three separate passes, and on the third one the model reads all seven tokens to produce one.

On its own, it predicts nothing at all. the build failed because it is what makes timed the favourite. Everything you leave in front of a model is changing what comes out of it, whether you meant it to or not.

The favourite is not always what comes out. Those scores are a distribution and the model samples from it, so the second row is taken often enough that the same question can give you a different answer twice in a row. None of the branches above were impossible. They were only less likely.

This is also why output is priced above input. A question arrives in one pass. An answer is built a token at a time, and each one is paid for with a full read of everything before it. The model unit puts numbers on that gap.

It reads all of it, every turn

Inside every one of those passes, the model does not look anything up. Here is the part people do not expect: each token is weighed against every token in front of it, and that whole weighing is thrown away and redone on the next pass. There is no index, and no shortcut for the parts that did not change.

This is the sentence you just watched it write, finished. Hold it and the heaviest line runs back to build, which is the answer you would have given. Hold the first token and nothing is drawn at all. A token only ever looks backwards.

Seven tokens make twenty-one pairs. Double the tokens and it is ninety-one, not forty-two. The reading grows faster than the thing being read, which is why a long session gets slower as well as dearer, and why the cheapest token is the one you never put in .

Looking only backwards has one useful consequence. Adding to the end leaves every earlier weighing exactly as it was, so the front of the pile can be kept and charged at a fraction next turn. That is what a cache is here. The harness unit prices it.
