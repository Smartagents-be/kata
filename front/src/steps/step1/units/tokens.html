Before anything reaches the model, your text is cut into chunks and every chunk is swapped for a number. Those chunks are tokens.

A token is the unit a language model works in. Everything an agent knows when it answers you is text, and that text is counted in tokens: how much fits, what it costs, and what the model reads again every time. Not in words, and not in characters.

For ordinary English, roughly 4 to 6 characters go into 1 token, so a page of text comes to a few hundred tokens.

Reading

Text does not split into words

A tokeniser has a fixed vocabulary, built once from a pile of text and then frozen. It cuts your input into the longest pieces that vocabulary already knows, so common English words come through whole. Anything it has not seen often enough gets taken apart until what is left does fit. Every provider ships its own vocabulary, so the boundaries differ in detail. The shape does not.

Plain text and code cost about the same number of tokens per character. Ids and hashes cost nearly 3 times as much, because the tokeniser recognises nothing in them. So a pasted stack trace or a log full of ids costs a lot more than you would think.

The vocabulary comes from the pile the model was trained on, so what breaks into fragments is also what it has read least of. For a big language like Dutch the difference is small. The sentence at the top of this page is 14 tokens in Dutch against 11 in English, and Anthropic measures answers in German or French at about 97% of the English. The gap is wider for a language with little text online, and wider again for your own class names.

Attention

In He deposits his money at the bank, bank could mean a river bank or an institution that handles money. The words deposits and money help identify the right meaning. The model weighs information from other tokens to make such connections. That weighing is called attention.

Every token gets 3 vectors: a query, a key and a value. They serve different roles. When a token seeks information from other tokens, its query expresses what it needs. When other tokens attend to this token, its key expresses what it offers. Its value contains the information it actually contributes.

For bank, the model compares its query with the keys of every earlier token and itself. It calculates their dot product: multiply the numbers at corresponding positions in the 2 vectors, then add the results. Each comparison produces a score.

The scores are converted into weights that add up to 100%. The higher the score, the more the corresponding value contributes. The model multiplies each value-vector by its weight and adds the vectors together, position by position. That lets information from deposits and money weigh heavily in understanding bank.

During training, the model learns how to calculate these vectors to make useful connections. When it writes, it uses those learned calculations.

The model runs several such weighings side by side and repeats the process. The figure shows 1 such weighing, with made-up weights and attention to the token itself left out. That gives 21 pairs for 7 tokens; 14 tokens would already give 91. The number of connections grows much faster than the text. Without a cache, the model does it all again on every turn, over the whole window.

A cache makes that cheaper, not free. What was already in the window is not worked out again, and you pay for it at a tenth of the input price or less. But you pay that every turn, for the whole window, and every new token still has to be weighed against everything before it. So a long session gets slower and dearer, cache or no cache. Writing

It writes 1 token at a time

Given everything it has been handed so far, the model scores the tokens that could come next, and one of them is taken. That token is stuck on the end, and the whole lot goes back in. Again.

On its own, it predicts nothing at all. the build failed because it makes timed the favourite. Everything you leave in front of a model changes what comes out of it, whether you meant it to or not.

The favourite is not always what comes out. Those scores are probabilities, and the model picks one according to them, so ask the same question 2 times and you can get 2 different answers. None of the other words above was impossible. They were only less likely.

The expensive part

Output is priced above input. The model reads your question in 1 go. It writes the answer a token at a time: every new token is another full pass through the network, and it cannot start until the token before it is there. How much dearer that makes output is in the model unit.

Thinking is writing too. Reasoning is billed as output, including the reasoning you never see: what reaches your screen is at most a summary, and the bill is for the whole thing. Here is 1 turn deep in a session, with the reading and the writing side by side.
