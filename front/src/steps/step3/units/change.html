Producing code is not the job any more. An agent writes it faster than you do and it does not get bored on the thirtieth call site. What is left is the part that was always harder: deciding what should exist, and finding out whether what came back is right.

You are not the only one who has to change. The person who wrote the requirement, the person who approves the release, the manager who wants to know what all this bought: each of them is standing next to a bottleneck that moved.

You are the test engineer

Reading every line stops scaling somewhere in the second hour of diff. What scales is the case you thought of and the agent did not. A book returned twice. A return dated before the loan. A member deleted while their statement was being rendered.

So write those down before the implementation exists. The quality gates in the engineering unit put a floor and a ceiling under whatever the run produced, and the scenarios you add on top are the ones nobody reaches from the happy path. Thinking of them is slow, which is the point. It is the part of the day worth being slow at.

Business moves closer

Something that used to take three weeks now has a working version by Thursday. That only helps if somebody who knows what it was for looks at it on Thursday. A prototype starts a conversation, and the conversation has to keep up with the prototype.

Which puts business in the loop weekly rather than at the demo, and pulls you some way into their half of the job. You end up knowing why a loan renews at fourteen days and not thirty. That is not scope creep. It is the only way to tell whether the thing that got built is the thing somebody wanted.

The process was the bottleneck

Management hears about agents and works out a multiple. Ten times the output, or five, or whatever the article said. It does not arrive, and the reason is not that the tool underdelivered. Typing was never what the calendar was waiting on.

Take the typing out and the calendar barely moves. Three days for a review, a fortnight for an environment, a release window on the last Thursday of the month. None of that got faster, and there is more going through it than there was. Enablement makes the case for one person carrying more of the stack, and every handover that removes is a queue that stops existing. The rest of it survives because it is the part everybody already knows how to do.

Code got cheap

Perfect code was worth paying for when writing it cost a week, and it cost a week because a person typed it. Regenerating a module from a sharper description costs an afternoon. So the code is the cheap half now, and the thinking that produced it is the expensive one.

Let quality drift where drift is survivable. A naming convention you would once have blocked a merge over is a five minute rewrite, and the attention that frees up goes on whether the domain is modelled right. Spec-driven work already keeps the description rather than the output. The gates stay where they are. What relaxes is taste.

The environment beats the project

Whatever ships this quarter matters less than what the next four people can do on Monday. An environment where humans and agents both work well is the deliverable: a CLAUDE.md that says how this repository does things, a skill for the job everybody does badly by hand, a build that is green on a fresh clone. Project setup is where each of those gets built.
