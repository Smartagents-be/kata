Producing code is not the job any more. An agent writes it faster than you do and it does not get bored on the 30th call site. What is left is the part that was always harder: deciding what should exist, and finding out whether what came back is right.

You are not the only one who has to change. The person who wrote the requirement, the person who approves the release, the manager who wants to know what all this bought: each of them is standing next to a bottleneck that moved.

You are the test engineer

Reading every line stops scaling somewhere in the second hour of diff. Writing down the case the agent did not think of does scale. A book returned 2 times. A return dated before the loan. A member deleted while their statement was being rendered.

So write those down before the implementation exists. The quality gates in the gates unit put a floor under whatever the run produced, and the scenarios you add on top are the ones nobody reaches from the happy path. Thinking of them is slow. That is the part of the day worth being slow at.

Business moves closer

Something that used to take 3 weeks now has a working version by Thursday. That only helps if somebody who knows what it was for looks at it on Thursday. A prototype starts a conversation, and the conversation has to keep up with the prototype.

Which puts business in the loop weekly rather than at the demo, and pulls you some way into their half of the job. You end up knowing why staff get 7 days of grace and an ordinary member gets none. That is not scope creep. It is the only way to tell whether the thing that got built is the thing somebody wanted.

Where a person still decides

The third row is 1 run with no handovers, and a person still decides 4 times: the intent, the spec, the change and the release. In between, the work runs without you, the way you hand it over in step 2's unit on long runs. Those 4 places are where the people at the top of this page stand now.

The process was the bottleneck

Management hears about agents and works out a multiple. 10 times the output, or 5, or whatever the article said. It does not arrive, and the reason is not that the tool underdelivered. Typing was never what the calendar was waiting on.

Take the typing out and the calendar barely moves. 3 days for a review, a fortnight for an environment, a release window on the last Thursday of the month. None of that got faster, and there is more going through it than there was. Step 2's unit on the feedback loop makes the case for 1 person carrying more of the stack, and every handover it removes is a queue that stops existing. The rest of it survives because it is the part everybody already knows how to do.

The way of working is a decision

Every practice you have was shaped by a constraint, and some of those constraints are gone. The unit on skills and scripts retires one of those practices at your own desk, where something done by hand turns into a script. Your team's way of working has the same leftovers, and nobody is going to point them out for you.

  • There is no PowerPoint in this course. The slides live in the repository and their drawings come from the same figure components the units render, so the picture on the projector cannot drift from the one on your screen. Nobody decided PowerPoint was bad. The constraint that made it the obvious choice went away.
  • Code review was built to catch what a tired person typed on a Friday. Ask what it is catching when an agent writes the diff and another agent reviews it. Not whether somebody reads, but what they are reading for.
  • A 2-week sprint was an honest batch when an increment took 2 weeks to produce. The skeleton now exists on Thursday. Whether Scrum is still the shape around that is a question your team is allowed to reopen.

Nobody has written down what replaces any of it. Being the one who asks is worth more to your organisation than anything you ship this week.

Code got cheap

Perfect code was worth paying for when writing it cost a week, and it cost a week because a person typed it. Regenerating a module from a sharper description costs an afternoon. So the code is the cheap half now, and the thinking that produced it is the expensive one.

Let quality drift where drift is survivable. A naming convention you would once have blocked a merge over is a 5-minute rewrite, and the attention that frees up goes on whether the domain is modelled right. Spec-driven work already keeps the description rather than the output. The gates stay where they are. What relaxes is taste.

The environment beats the project

Whatever ships this quarter matters less than what the next 4 people can do on Monday. An environment where humans and agents both work well is the deliverable: a CLAUDE.md that says how this repository does things, a skill for the job everybody does badly by hand, a build that is green on a fresh clone. The setup unit is where each of those gets built.

Whatever ships this quarter matters less than what the next 4 people can do on Monday. An environment where humans and agents both work well is the deliverable: a AGENTS.md that says how this repository does things, a skill for the job everybody does badly by hand, a build that is green on a fresh clone. The setup unit is where each of those gets built.

It is also the work nobody schedules. Count the times this week you told an agent something you had told it before Each one is a line missing from a file, and that line is the difference between a trick of yours and a team that works differently.
