Licences arrive in an afternoon and change nothing on their own. What has to change is how work gets handed over, what a reviewer does with a diff nobody typed, and where the team keeps its conventions. None of that came in the purchase.

So this is a change to habits rather than to a stack. A habit spreads when somebody watches it work on code they own themselves. Go looking for that first. It is worth more than any presentation you could give about it.

Start where the work already hurts

Pick the job nobody wants. The module with no tests, the migration that has sat on the board for two quarters, the thirty places a renamed field has to follow. Wide, mechanical and measurable is exactly the shape the unit on goals hands over well. Finish one of those and you have stopped arguing.

Skip the demo where an agent writes a small feature from nothing. Everybody has seen it, and it answers a question nobody in the room is asking. What they want to know is what happens to that code in review.

The sceptics are reading the diff

Resistance usually comes from whoever reviews. An agent that produces forty classes in an afternoon moves the bottleneck onto them, so your week got better and theirs got worse. That is a fair complaint, and enthusiasm is not an answer to it.

Checks are. A coverage floor, a complexity ceiling and a green build are what the quality gates in the engineering unit ask for anyway, and they mean a wall of diff has been measured before a human opens it. What is left for the reviewer is judgement, which is the half of reviewing anybody wanted.

Conventions live in the repository

A convention in one person's head is not a change to the team. It is a preference that person re-explains every sprint. Written into CLAUDE.md, or into a skill the agent reaches for on its own, it is something the next joiner inherits on their first clone, which is what project setup is for.
