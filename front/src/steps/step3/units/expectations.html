The people around you saw what you saw. A working version of something, on screen, an afternoon after somebody asked for it. Some of them did not ask at all: the tools are on their laptop too, and a manager can put a prototype together over lunch. What everybody took away is that everything now takes an afternoon.

The unit on small working increments already named what that version is: a skeleton with the details left out. The distance between it and something you would put in front of a customer is where the promises go wrong.

The tool is not the advantage

Management believes a bigger version of that: the tool bought the team an advantage. Your competitor is on the same models, in the same week, reading the same article about them. Whatever the tooling hands you, it hands them. The unit on team and process change already showed the promised speed-up not arriving. A gain everybody gets is the new floor rather than a lead.

A tool alone does not know your domain, current codebase or users. The context unit showed why those details matter. Your advantage is knowing which evidence and requirements the work needs, then checking what the tool produces.

Say what is missing

Show the rough version, then name what it does not do yet. No validation, 1 hard-coded member, nothing stored between refreshes. 1 sentence, and it buys the only thing you needed from that meeting: agreement about what everybody just looked at. When the prototype is theirs rather than yours, it is the same sentence and it is still yours to say.

Leave it out and the sentence still gets said. Later, by somebody asking why the feature they signed off on falls over on an empty list, and you will be explaining the same detail from a worse position.

The detail nobody specified

An agent implements the request it was given, and the request was shallow. Ask for book returns and that is what comes back, handled, tested, along the path you described. The time then goes on the cases nobody had in mind when that request was written, and that includes you. Some of them do not exist until the thing is half built.

From the outside that looks like work which finished on Thursday and then went on for 3 weeks anyway. Naming the thing you want gets you a sharper first draft, but not the detail nobody knew about yet. So the burden moved rather than lifted. It sits with the people who can find that detail, and finding it takes room in the week, not a date at the end of it.

The estimate still matters

Writing the code got faster. Deciding what to build did not, and neither did the deploy. Step 2's unit on owning the feedback cycle counts where the day goes, and typing was never the expensive part of it.

Then there is the reading, which is new work. An agent that finishes in an hour hands you an hour of diff you did not write, and code you did not write is slower to read than your own. Steering makes the same point about running 2 agents at once: they got faster and you did not. So when you give a date, give one with the checking inside it, or "done" is going to mean 2 different things in one room.

1 good run is not a velocity

Hand the same request over 2 times and it does not come back the same 2 times. The unit on reusing procedures and scripts draws that spread and then puts a script around the part that should not be varying at all. Until you have done that, the afternoon that went well is 1 sample.

You used to correct a number like that by instinct, and that correction is gone. You estimated from years of having done the work, and your manager read the quality off knowing who was on the team; both are readings of a job that changed shape underneath them. Everybody is recalibrating at once, so the honest promise is the next checkpoint rather than the finish. A skeleton on Thursday, a green build on Monday. A date that assumed every run lands has nowhere to go the first time one does not.
