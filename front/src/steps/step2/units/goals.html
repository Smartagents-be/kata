Some ways of working burn a day of tokens in an afternoon. That is not automatically waste. A run that costs ten ordinary sessions and hands back a module that had no tests this morning is a trade worth making. The moves below are the expensive ones: a goal you walk away from, a fleet of agents, a long turn on the frontier model, a design pass. What decides it is what you get for the spend, and when you spend it.

When matters because of how you are billed. Step 1's unit on the model set out the two arrangements: a key bills you per token, a subscription comes off a plan you paid for before you started. Where a plan meters you in a rolling window, usually about five hours, what you leave unused is not carried anywhere. Forty minutes of window is not something you can save for tomorrow, so the thing to do with a window is spend it out. The limit that actually binds sits behind it, weekly or monthly depending on the plan, and that is the one to keep an eye on.

Goal-oriented

A goal is a task with an outcome something else can measure. Say what has to be true when the work is done, not which keys to press. "Move the em-dash rule out of my head and into the repo" leaves room for a good answer. "Add a paragraph to CLAUDE.md" has already picked one, and if a skill was the better home you will never hear about it. An instruction leaves you one thing to check: whether you were obeyed.

A goal without edges drifts, so the request still carries them. Name the outcome, name what must not change, name how you will check it. The middle is the agent's to work out. 

> Goal: every method under article/ scores 10 or lower on
  cyclomatic complexity. Run `mvn verify` after each class you
  change, keep it green, and do not change a public signature.

Hand one of those over and the agent stops asking you things. It does a pass, runs your check, and goes back in on whatever failed. It comes back when the check passes or when it is properly stuck. You are not in that loop, and that is both the point and the price.

The check is the exit

So the check has to be something that answers on its own. "Ninety percent line coverage" is a number the build prints. "Cleaner code" is not. If you cannot name the command that answers yes or no, you do not have a goal yet, and four hours later you get a confident report on a wish.

When the goal is the whole job

Some goals are far too big for one turn and still fit in one sentence. You inherit a service written six years ago, in production, with almost no tests. What you hand over is not "write a test for OrderService". It is "get this module to ninety percent line coverage and leave mvn test green", and then you walk away from it.

Code that was vibe-coded has its own version of that. A folder grew fast, nobody read it, and now the methods are five levels deep. Do not hand over a cleanup list. Hand over a ceiling: no method above a cyclomatic complexity of ten. Wire that check into the build first, because a limit the build cannot measure is a limit nobody can hold the agent to.

Both of those run for hours and burn tokens the whole time. Four hours is not unusual, and you gave up the steering a short session gives you, because nobody is watching step two while it happens. What comes back is work that ran your check until the check said yes. For a job that is mechanical and wide, and that a command can measure, that is a good trade.

Most of it is waiting

Look at where those four hours actually go. The agent writes a class in seconds. mvn verify then takes four minutes, and the pass after it takes four more, and forty passes later the build has had nearly three hours of it. Tokens are burning the whole time, but the clock is being run by your own test suite.

Which is why this shape is worth starting before you need it. You changed a rule in CLAUDE.md, so the goal is the codebase catching up with it everywhere, in one run. Start it in the morning and read it after lunch Nothing in that job needs you in the room, and the hours it costs are not hours off your day.

Give it its own worktree

Meanwhile you still have a day of your own to get through. An agent rewriting forty classes for four hours is touching the files you have open, which makes your branch unusable while it runs. Ask for the goal to be pursued in a git worktree instead, the second checkout the steering unit gives every agent. 

git worktree add -b goal/complexity ../kata-complexity

Then merge back in pieces instead of at the end. One module finished and green is a merge worth doing, and it means you read a module of diff while the next one is still being written. It caps the damage too. A run that goes wrong in hour three has not taken the first two hours down with it.

Ultracode

Ask for ultracode and the harness stops working the task itself. It writes a script that fans agents out across the work, runs them, and gathers up what comes back. You are not handing over one job. You are handing over a fleet.

It pays when you already know what you want built. Write the specs first, the way the workflows unit describes, one per piece of work, and let the fleet implement them at the same time. Five specs, five agents, an afternoon of work arriving at once. With nothing written down it is five agents guessing in parallel, and you read five wrong answers instead of one.

Count what that costs before you reach for it. Every agent starts on an empty context, so every one of them reads its way into the project again: the same CLAUDE.md, the same files, the same working out. You pay for that reading once per agent, and none of them learn anything from the others. It is the least efficient way to spend tokens in this unit, and it is also the fastest way to get five things done.

Research on the frontier model

Above the three sits the frontier model. The model unit called it a ceiling rather than a fourth tier and stopped there, because what it is good at is a question about work rather than about windows. Broad research is the answer. Point it at something with a lot of surface, a migration nobody here has done or a library nobody has read, and what comes back is worth more than the same hours from the tier below.

Then do not let it write the code. Read what it found, take the part that survives into a planning session on the top tier, and hand that plan down to the middle tier to implement, turn after turn Each step down costs less per token than the one above it, and the frontier model is spent on the one thing only it does. You are the digestion in the middle, and that is the part of the chain nobody can hand over.

Design tools

A design tool is the same spend in a different shape. Claude Design takes a brief and builds a whole design system out of it, then redraws the thing while you watch: every colour, every component, every state, over and over. The teal on this page, its two typefaces and every token behind them came out of one of those sessions.

What it costs is a lot, quickly. Each pass renders the whole system again, and you are not steering it sentence by sentence the way you steer a coding session. Half an hour of it empties a window that a morning of careful work barely touched. So it belongs in the same slot as the fleet, near the end of a window, on something you actually want redrawn.

What you are left with

Every move in this unit hands you work you did not watch being made. A fleet that ran while you were at lunch, a design system you did not draw, forty classes rewritten by a run you left alone. Spending the tokens is the easy half. The workshop is where you write one of these goals for real and let the build say whether you hit it.
