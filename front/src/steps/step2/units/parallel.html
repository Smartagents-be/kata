One agent at a time

One agent, one conversation, and you read every step it takes. No arrangement gives you more control than that. You see the file it opened before anything is written, and you answer a wrong turn while it still costs one sentence. The diff that comes back fits in your head. The steering unit is about what you do inside that window.

Reach for it when there is one thing you care about. A late-fee rule that rounds the wrong way, a query that got slow, a domain model you are still arguing with yourself about. That work is deep, and depth is where your attention buys more than throughput does. Nothing else is running, so nothing else is asking for you.

Many agents at once

Open four sessions and you get four times the output and a quarter of the attention. Nobody reads four runs as they happen, so you stop steering and start receiving. Everything you gave up at the start comes back as reading at the end. All at once, in four places, on work you did not watch being made.

It pays when the work splits cleanly and each piece has a check of its own. Four modules, four builds that go green or red without you. Give each agent its own git worktree, which steering argues for, or they edit the same files and you find out at merge time.

The ceiling you hit first is you. Four streams of output in an afternoon is more than anyone reads carefully, and the tenth diff gets less of you than the first did. You run out of attention well before you run out of tokens.

The orchestrator

There is a better-managed version of that. You talk to one agent, and it hands the pieces to sub-agents it briefs itself Step 1's unit on the harness called this the coordinator and worked out what it costs. It moves the coordination. In the agent, not in your head.

The builder and the critic

A builder implements the change and a separate critic reports concrete defects against the requirements and check results. The builder addresses confirmed findings and repeats the review within a fixed limit. This extends the reflection pattern from the harness unit. You review the resulting diff and unresolved findings.

So the whole arrangement has to be in the prompt. Nobody is there to answer once it starts, and the bar you give the critic is the last thing you get to say about what good looks like. 

Build a link shortener, in a project of its own. POST /api/links takes
a URL and answers with a code, GET /api/links/{code} hands the URL
back, and a page lists what is in there. I want it as something I
would hand a colleague: codes that do not collide, an expiry, and a
URL that gets checked. Keep it as plain as TitleController in
kata/step1/java, which is the house style for a controller here.

You implement it. Fan out sub-agents for the parts that do not depend
on each other. Then spawn one more as a harsh critic, hand it the diff
and the running app, and tell it to attack the design, the naming and
the cases nobody thought about. It reports back. It fixes nothing.

Repeat review and repair for at most 3 rounds. Done requires a green
mvn verify and no confirmed blocking findings against the requirements.
Stop at the limit or if blocked, and report unresolved findings.
Return the diff and check results for my review; do not publish.

Strip the wording off a prompt like that and the same pieces are left.

  • Intent and a goal. What you want, and what would make it good. The second half is the one that gets left out, and it is the half the critic works from.
  • Separate briefs. Delegate independent parts and give the critic the requirements, current diff and check results. A separate review can reduce anchoring but still needs verification.
  • A critic that is harsh. Attack the design, the naming and the cases nobody thought about, report back, repair nothing. An agent asked to help will happily help you finish the wrong thing.
  • A completion condition and a limit. Require passing checks and no confirmed blocking findings. Stop after 3 review rounds and report anything unresolved for human review.
  • A gold standard. Point at something that is already good, the way the prompt above points at TitleController. "Better" is an opinion two agents can argue about all afternoon. "As good as this file" is a comparison.
Nothing else in this unit is this slow or this expensive. Two hours is an ordinary run, and every round pays for the whole design being read and written again. Keep it for work that is walled off from everything else and has to come back good.

Repeated reviews can expose missed cases, but they also spend tokens and can reinforce a shared mistake. Read the final diff, check results and unresolved findings before deciding whether to ship.

One interactive agent, background goals

One active agent stays out in front on the build, while the other agents handle preparation, analysis and verification in the background.

The one in front you work with the way you would if it were the only one, answering it and reading the diff it hands back. The ones behind are running goals that take an hour and need nothing from you until they report. Middling control, and most days look like this.

Scaffolding a new domain is the shape of it. Behind you, one agent is writing tests against the part you settled an hour ago while another brings the docs up to date. Up front you and the agent are still arguing the model out and writing the spec that comes of it. The agents behind you are running goals rather than instructions, so you can leave them alone. The unit on long-running goals and cost is where that shape is argued.
