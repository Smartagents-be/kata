One agent at a time

One agent, one conversation, and you read every step it takes. No arrangement gives you more control than that. You see the file it opened before anything is written, and you answer a wrong turn while it still costs one sentence. The diff that comes back fits in your head. The steering unit is about what you do inside that window.

Reach for it when there is one thing you actually care about. A late-fee rule that rounds the wrong way, a query that got slow, a domain model you are still arguing with yourself about. That work is not wide. It is deep, and depth is where your attention buys more than throughput does. Nothing else is running, so nothing else is asking for you.

Many agents at once

Open four sessions and you get four times the output and a quarter of the attention. Nobody reads four runs as they happen, so you stop steering and start receiving. Everything you gave up at the start comes back as reading at the end. All at once, in four places, on work you did not watch being made.

It pays when the work splits cleanly and each piece has a check of its own. Four modules, four builds that go green or red without you. Give each agent its own git worktree, which steering argues for, or they edit the same files and you find out at merge time.

The ceiling you hit first is you. Four streams of output in an afternoon is more than anyone reads carefully, and the tenth diff gets less of you than the first did. Attention is what you run out of, well before the tokens.

The orchestrator

There is a better-managed version of that. You talk to one agent, and it hands the pieces to sub-agents it briefs itself Step 1's unit on the harness called this the coordinator and worked out what it costs. What matters here is where it puts the coordination. In the agent, not in your head.

The builder and the critic

Two agents can also be pointed at each other. One builds, the other takes the work apart, and the builder goes back in carrying the remarks. Nothing stops it after one pass: it builds again, gets taken apart again, and keeps going until the critic has nothing left to say. That is the reflection pattern from step 1's unit on the harness, run as a loop instead of once. You are in none of the rounds.

So the whole arrangement has to be in the prompt. Nobody is there to answer once it starts, and the bar you give the critic is the last thing you get to say about what good looks like. 

Build a link shortener, in a project of its own. POST /api/links takes
a URL and answers with a code, GET /api/links/{code} hands the URL
back, and a page lists what is in there. I want it as something I
would hand a colleague: codes that do not collide, an expiry, and a
URL that gets checked. Keep it as plain as TitleController in
kata/step1/java, which is the house style for a controller here.

You implement it. Fan out sub-agents for the parts that do not depend
on each other. Then spawn one more as a harsh critic, hand it the diff
and the running app, and tell it to attack the design, the naming and
the cases nobody thought about. It reports back. It fixes nothing.

/loop over this until the critic has no remarks left and is wowed by
what it is looking at. Done is a green mvn verify and a critic that
cannot fault it.

Under the wording, the same pieces are always in there.

  • Intent and a goal. What you want, and what would make it good. The second half is the one that gets left out, and it is the half the critic works from.
  • Sub-agents to fan out to. The parts that do not depend on each other go out in parallel, and the critic is a fresh one of its own. An agent turning around to look at its own afternoon agrees with itself.
  • A critic that is harsh. Attack the design, the naming and the cases nobody thought about, report back, repair nothing. An agent asked to help will happily help you finish the wrong thing.
  • A bar the builder has to clear. Done is not "it runs". Done is the critic having no remarks left, and the loop is what closes that gap.
  • A golden standard. Point at something that is already good, the way the prompt above points at TitleController. "Better" is an opinion two agents can argue about all afternoon. "As good as this file" is a comparison.
Nothing else in this unit is this slow or this expensive. Two hours is an ordinary run, and every round pays for the whole design being read and written again. Keep it for work that is walled off from everything else and has to come back good.

What you buy for that is rounds. Many passes over one problem instead of one pass over five, and none of them needs you, so the whole thing runs unattended while you are somewhere else. It reports once, with the critic's last word beside the diff, which is a thing you can actually read. What comes back is usually work you would have signed off yourself.

One in front, the rest behind

Keep one agent in front of you and hand the long jobs to agents behind it. The one in front you work with the way you would if it were the only one, answering it and reading the diff it hands back. The ones behind are running goals that take an hour and need nothing from you until they report. Middling control, and it is what most days actually look like.

Scaffolding a new domain is the shape of it. Behind you, one agent is writing tests against the part you settled an hour ago while another brings the docs up to date. Neither needs you. Up front you and the agent are still arguing the model out and writing the spec that comes of it. What is behind you are goals rather than instructions, which is what makes them safe to leave alone, and the unit on goals is where that shape is argued.
