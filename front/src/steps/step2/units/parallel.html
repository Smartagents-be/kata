One agent at a time

One agent, one conversation, and you read every step it takes. That is the arrangement with the most control in it. You see the file it opened before it writes anything, you answer a wrong turn while it is still a sentence, and the diff that comes back is one diff you can hold in your head. The steering unit is all about that window.

Reach for it when there is one thing you actually care about. A late-fee rule that rounds the wrong way, a query that got slow, a domain model you are still arguing with yourself about. That work is not wide, it is deep, and depth is where your attention buys more than throughput does. Nothing else is running, so nothing else is asking for you.

Many agents at once

Open four sessions and you get four times the output and a quarter of the attention. Nobody reads four runs as they happen, so you stop steering and start receiving. Everything you gave up at the start comes back as reading at the end, and it arrives all at once, in four places, on work you did not watch being made.

So it pays when the work splits cleanly and each piece has a check of its own. Four modules, four builds that go green or red without you. Give each agent its own git worktree, which steering argues for, or they edit the same files and you find out at merge time.

Two agents on the same code write over each other, and the more of them you run the likelier that gets. The other ceiling is you. Four streams of output in an afternoon is more than anyone reads carefully, and the tenth diff gets less of you than the first did. Attention is what you run out of, well before the tokens.

The orchestrator

There is a better-managed version of that. You talk to one agent, and it hands the pieces to sub-agents it briefs itself Step 1's unit on the harness called this the coordinator and worked out what it costs. What matters here is where it puts the coordination: in the agent, rather than in your head.

One in front, the rest behind

Keep one agent in front of you and hand the long jobs to agents behind it. The one in front you work with the way the first section describes, answering it and checking what it lands. The ones behind are running goals that take an hour and need nothing from you until they report. Middling control, and it is the most of the day most people get.

Scaffolding a new domain is the shape of it. You and the agent in front settle the names, argue the model, write the spec. Behind that, one agent is writing tests against the part you settled an hour ago and another is bringing the docs up to date. Those are goals rather than instructions, which is what makes them safe to leave alone, and the unit on goals is where that shape is argued.
